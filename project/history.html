Ice Cream History

The Beginning...

Dairy farming has been a practice since ancient times, and nearly every inhabited part of the globe will experience freezing temperatures at the proper elevation and season. As a result, there is understandable dispute over what nation first made ice cream. There are documented cases of ice and snow being used for desserts in ancient Rome, China, and Persia; ancient Persians even built ice houses called yakhchāl.

However, the definition of ice cream matters. While it's credible that ice and milk were anciently consumed together, frozen dairy seems to be a rarity, especially in pure forms, and probably for good reason; even with high levels of fat and sugar, dairy that is simply frozen will typically become a prohibitively solid mass, even when using a container that will readily unmold its contents (imagine making popsicles in a clay pot!). Even disregarding dairy content, it could be argued that even sorbets or sherbets as we know them (the sharbat from which both terms originate is a beverage) did not truly exist until methods were invented for churning the dessert while it was being frozen.

The first recorded instances of this appear to be in Renaissance Italy, dating back to at least 1560 AD. The Italian origin is perhaps why it is has been said that ice cream was introduced to Europe from China via Marco Polo, a claim which has no support from the surviving accounts of his travels. Regardless, the concept is thought to have spread from Italy to France before reaching England, with ice cream consumption seeming to be largely a luxury of royalty for some time. The "debut" of ice cream could perhaps be attributed to Francesco Procopio dei Coltelli, a Sicilian chef who opened the Café Procope in France, 1686, serving the public coffee, lemonade, and soft frozen desserts with and without dairy.

From Cafés to Soda Fountains

While ice cream and the methods for making it were public knowledge by the 18th Century, modern refrigeration was not. Ice still had to be harvested from natural sources, and having it on hand entailed specialized transportation and storage. While the wealthy of the time might have the ability to make and even store ice cream from the convenience of home on a whim, the average person would likely have to visit some place of business to enjoy ice cream.

A local tavern might have served it, as might a candy shop, and, like the cafés of Europe, the coffee shops of the Western Hemisphere might easily carry frozen goods.

Penny Licks and Hokey Pokey

While in the 19th century, modern refrigeration was still in the works, the ice trade had seen massive improvements. Perhaps this enabled the thriving trade of ice cream street vendors which arose.

The Present State

The end of wartime rationing and the homecoming of America's troops seemed to bring about an explosion in the ice cream industry, and the people of the United States continue to consume frozen desserts almost as a way of life. However, that is not to say that every part of the industry has thrived; in fact, many businesses are now nothing more than nostalgic memories.

There are, perhaps, two developments to blame for this. The first is modern refrigeration, which enables virtually everyone (and every grocery store) to maintain a constant and convenient stockpile of ice cream to be consumed without the need to leave the house. The other potential agent of change, operating hand-in-hand with the former, is the explosion of bespoke flavors. Diverse flavors of ice cream have existed since the dessert's invention, and while there exists a familiar refrain of "in my day, there were only three flavors: vanilla, chocolate, and strawberry," there is no shortage of black-and-white photographs of menus behind ice cream counters to prove otherwise. Nevertheless, the industry seems to have undergone a major shift since Irv Robbins and Burt Baskin returned from the second World War and began selling ice cream. And while their changing roster of 31 flavors may have originally only been available for tasting in-store, Ben Cohen and Jerry Greenfield ("Ben & Jerry") have ensured that the sort of agglomeration of toppings one might previously received hand-assembled in a sundae would now be sold pre-packaged and ready to scoop at every supermarket.

While storefronts for ice cream do persist, their market share seems to have largely dwindled down to major corporations, with struggling competition from sparse local establishments. Walgreens no longer splits bananas, soda fountains are a thing of the past. Soft-serve desserts at least necessitate a storefront, and the hype over the more nutritionally marketable frozen yogurt encouraged a boom in such, but the fad seems to have definitively passed. While the ice cream truck held its ground a bit longer than the more "retro" establishments, the Good Humor company sold off its fleet, and the rare menu-encrusted truck that does jangle its way through a modern neighborhood is perhaps received with more mockery and suspicion than excitement.

Tastes have also apparently changed. Sundaes of the past commonly contained fruit and nuts as toppings, but chocolate, candy, and baked goods have largely displaced them from the ingredient list. Now the rare, token banana split on the menu may have sprinkles rather than peanuts, and the pinapple will likely be replaced with the less-divisive caramel sauce.

However, this isn't to say that taste and craftsmanship in ice cream have vanished. Amid the search for novelty in flavors and healthy alternatives, there has been increasing interest in quality ingredients and good texture, and hobbyists now learn how to make their own ice cream from home.
